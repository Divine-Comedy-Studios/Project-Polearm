This final chapter reflects on the technical development of Project Polearm, examining not only the systems we created, but also the difficulties and limitations we encountered while building them. It begins by discussing our approach to debugging and testing, including the use of dedicated testing environments such as the Gym, and acknowledges our relatively informal testing practices. The development of the custom FloatingBody character controller and the EnemyManager responsible for coordinating enemy encounters represent our biggest challenges throughout development and a look back is taken to their the root of the troubles. Finally, after demonstrating the strenghts of our philosophies in previous chapters, the less successful parts of our development process equally reveal their weaknesses. Investing in modularity and configurability often made later development considerably cleaner and faster, but sometimes introduced unnecessary complexity and delayed overall progress, which atlas leads us to our conclusion of what we would do differently in the future.

## 7.1. Debugging and Testing
---
As the feature set of our game is, in comparison to AAA games at least, not the biggest, we could get away with less rigorous testing practices than what is more commonly seen in larger studios. That meant, that for most features, no separate testing environment was developed before they were utilized, or that systems were developed sequencially such that one subsystem will always be debugged before development is continued. Integration testing was our main way of edging out bugs and other issues.

There were some examples to this rule. In early development, a so called "gym" was created to test player mechanics, especially the capabilities of the `FloatingBody`. The gym was a large, open area where obstacles were placed that were used to test for edge-case scenarios and test the general game feel and configuration of the player. Ledges and small overhangs were repeatedly run over and stopped at to guarantee that our custom physics works and felt as expected.

The gym was used until the very end of development to test enemy encounters, as its openess proved to be quite useful for isolating unwanted behavior in a clean environment, and its status as a testing ground made any quick changes to, f.e. enemy encounter behavior a one-time thing. Furthermore, the role switching mechanism of the `EnemyManager` was also tested in the gym by having the enemies repeatedly circling the player, who for the test, we set to have an obsurdly large amount of health, and attacking them.

Debugging throughout development was also rather primitive. As the game is simple, no use of profilers or similar tools were needed, and even the debugger was used not too commonly, as print debugging was often enough for our purposes, as it is quick to set up and quick to remove. The large amount of centralization in systems also meant that bugs in the systems can be quickly fixed due to the large amount of dependencies applying them universally. For decentralized components such as entity actions, as they implement specialized behavior debugging is also simple, as they are isolated and can only affect other systems in imited and controlled ways.

The lack of proper testing also has to do with our lack of a proper workflow for establishing different environments in which every systems can be tested in isolation. To test API-based systems, one has to establish a testing environment, where time is spent developing test cases by calling said API. We most often did not find the time or patience to do this, as we can simply test its functionality by inserting it into its proper use case. The time was instead invested into other parts of the project or into polish for the same system. For future endeavors, exploring rigous testing practices may prove to be insightful undertaking.

## 7.2. Challenges during Development
---
Both of the team members have never worked on such an extensive and long-winding project before. It is therefore expected, that many technical problems and challenges will arise along the way. Many of our choices made during development, no matter concrete design philosophies or architectual decisions, were all made to avoid technical debt and issues as best as possible, but avoiding them alltogether has proven to have not been possible for us. These are some of our largest challenges we have encountered during the 11-month timespan:

### 7.2.1. A Dream of Stepping up Stairs
---
The `FloatingBody` was the first big roadblock in the development of the Project. Before we started development on the project proper, a small test in Godot was created to simply explore the capabilities and limitations of the engine, where we very quickly so came to the realization that Godot's default `CharacterBody3D` could possibly prove to not be enough for our purposes, as it oftentimes got stuck on even small edges in the ground, where a player might expect to be able to simply walk over them. Furthermore, the observation occurred to us that in many newer games, characters can move across extremely uneven ground with obstacles jutting out, f.e. stairs, with extreme smoothness, which a naïve collision resolution algorithm could not do.

Although we were aware that one could simply use slopes as the collision shape for stairs and avoid having any spots jutting out from the ground, this meant that we would have to have a trade-off between the level designer and code-complexity, which was not one that we thought to be acceptable.

Due to our supposed insight for future troubles and the aim to avoid them, the development on the FloatingBody was started. As demonstrated in section 5.5, it became one of the most complex single algorithms in the project. The only resources on this topic we found during the time of the development were a few reddit posts, a single GitHub repository and a YouTube video (which used the latter method in a bouncy, physics-based game instead of an snappy action game) all demonstrating a technique of a collision body floating ontop of a raycast. Although that sounds like much, it was still far from a tutorial we could base a custom implementation off. In the end, the GitHub repository became the main source of inspiration, although only a limited amount of information was and could be gathered due to the difference in engine and our aim to strive for our own solution to the problem.

The development process was arduous, and the algorithm went through several iterations before landing on its final form, with some iterations being arguably more complex, but less smooth. In the end, the source code for the `FloatingBody` was to be divided into three files as its length became a point of confusion. And yet, our demo-level in the end proved to be less complicated than what the `FloatingBody` was built for. This is one such example of where our philosophy for encompassing as many future changes as possible backfired.

The sources we used in the development of the class are documented in the respective code files.

### 7.2.2.  The Troubles of Managing Enemies
---
The `EnemyManager` also utilized a very complicated and rather sophisticated algorithm. The entire system surrounding the spawning of enemies was inspired from _Ninja Gaiden Sigma Plus_, one of the games that we analized during this project, where it seemed to us that foes could take up different roles. For more information, please refer to the main accompanying report.

With that information in mind, we set out in an attempt at recreating a similar system, and also in accordance with our philosophy of _Configure Everything, Code Nothing_, the system was supposed to, once finished, do everything automatically. The only thing which needs to be done would constitute a creation of a tiny trigger script for encounters as well as the configuration of the actual contents of the encounter itself, and minimal coding is required.

The development of the `EnemyManager` and adjacent systems went on for roughly three months according to the project diary, not only because the enemy manager was extremely long and complicated, but mainly due to procrastination.  The enemy manager was, in a sense, the heart of the gameplay, one of the core aspects of the project, as its correct and useful implementation is essential to answering our research question. Therefore, extreme consideration was taken to ensure a future proof system, so extreme in fact, that almost productive work was done for a long time due to the fear of making a lesser implementation, time, which could have been spent writing the system multiple times over again in itself. The final implementation was finally finished in roughly a week of concentrated development time, where it finally occurred to us that progress may not stall longer to achieve our deadline successfully.

The final algorithm, having even gone through one major redesign, was not written with elegancy or much extendability in mind, but it turned out to be fine, usable and the code was quite clean. In hindsight, this enemy assignment algorithm wouldn't be very extendable anyways, as any major modification would mean rewriting parts of the algorithm, and there is only one enemy assignment algorithm anyways.

But the end, the engineering put into the `EnemyManager` paid off, as configuring enemies became a simple act of entering an array of enemy types into the editor, as well as configure some details surrounding the encounter, and the enemy manager worked its magic. But for future endeavors, it should also be important to recognize the dangers of overthinking future features and premature systems design.

### 7.2.3. Dead Ends
---
Both of the above troubles have one thing in common, which would be overencompassing and overthinking about future features. This problem is also present in some other parts of the codebase, although in smaller scope than the above two examples. There are hints towards incomplete features scattered throughout, and although they don't influence the correct execution of the game, they are still byproducts of this philosophy.

For example, the player states have a flag called `ENEMY_DETECTABLE`, which implemented for the idea that enemies could parry the players attacks or attack the player in vulnerable situations. This could not be implemented due to time and scope constraints. Entities also contain a property in their resources named `superArmor`. This was initially derived from a mechanic of the same name present in many hack-and-slash games, but ultimately didn't manage to be integrated into the final product. These are not the only examples that one may find in the codebase.

As mentioned in section 3.1., this planning for the future is a tradeoff, a tradeoff which has paid off multiple times, very incredibly so, but also one which has left parts of our code in debt. Sometimes, we were shocked at how quickly systems could be assembled together in a clean fashion. For example, the first time when attacking was properly added into the game, it was done so in a single day, as all other systems were already in place and prepared for the fact, or the fact that the first proper enemy in the demo was cleanly implemented in roughly three days. Other times, this philosophy lead to burnout, procrasination and dead ends in code, hinting at a plan not yet fulfilled.
